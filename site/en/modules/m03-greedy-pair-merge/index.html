Problem statement

Input

A weighted undirected graph (node size 1 by default), a target number of clusters K, and an optional capacity C.

Output

The clusters actually obtained, the cut, the number of clusters, whether K is reached, the stop reason, and a record of every merge.

Objective

Keep the wires between clusters (the cut) small by repeatedly merging the “closest” pair of clusters.

Constraints

Merge only pairs with affinity > 0; with a capacity, a merged cluster’s size may not exceed C; a merge is never undone.

Assumptions

Affinity = the total weight of every edge between the two clusters; the selection key (−affinity, members of A, members of B) is compared field by field.

Stopping

The number of clusters reaches K, or no legal pair with positive affinity remains (then say honestly that K is not reached; never force a merge).

§ 1Connections and local choices

Module 02 answered “who is closest to whom”. The most natural next step: put the closest two together first, then look at the next pair. A method that “only makes the best choice in sight at each step” is called greedy, and clustering that merges upward from single nodes is called agglomerative clustering.

Its strength is a simple rule that you can work by hand at every step; its weakness is just as clear: a merge is never split again, and early choices shape every later one. This chapter makes the rule precise first; the next (Module 04) is devoted to where it goes wrong.

§ 2Singletons, the target K and the affinity between clusters

cluster

簇

A group of nodes placed together. At the start, every node is a “singleton” cluster of its own.

contraction

收缩

Merging two clusters into one “supernode”: the wires between them become inside wires, and parallel wires to outside nodes add up.

target K

目标簇数

The number of clusters you want; stop once the count reaches K.

inter-cluster affinity

簇间亲和度

The total weight of every wire between two clusters. This module uses only this affinity.

Why is the affinity between clusters a “total weight”? Because when two clusters merge, every wire between them becomes an inside wire and no longer counts in the cut. So:

Every merge lowers the cut by exactly that merge’s affinity.

This property lets you read the effect of each step directly, and it is one of the properties this course’s tests check (it holds at every step on 200 random graphs).

§ 3Value first, then the tie rule

At each step we must pick, from all candidates, the pair that “most deserves” to merge. This course fixes the selection key:

(−affinity, members of cluster A, members of cluster B), compared field by field; take the smallest.

The first field is negated so that higher affinity comes first; the other two fields break ties by the members’ names in alphabetical order. For example, AB–C and D–E both score 5; compare the member lists [A, B] and [D]: A < D, so AB–C merges first.

The comparator bug on the original page (fixed in this course)

The original page’s JavaScript reads if (!best || cand < best), where cand is an array. When JavaScript compares two arrays it first turns them into strings: [-1, "A", "C"] becomes "-1,A,C" and [-5, "A", "B"] becomes "-5,A,B". The character 1 is less than 5, so A–C, with affinity 1, is treated as the “better” pair.

The result: on the same graph, the original page merges A–C first and ends at ACDE | B, cut 9; the correct result is ABC | DE, cut 3. Across four settings (K=2, K=3, capacity 2, capacity 3) the original page gives 9/16/12/16, and the correct values are 3/8/8/3. The original Python reference implementation is correct: the problem is only in the page’s JS.

This course’s engine uses compareKeys(), which compares field by field, and adds regression tests for 10 versus 2 and 2.5 versus 2 so that it cannot be “right by luck” on this five-node graph alone.

§ 4Step by step: AB → ABC → DE

On G01, take K = 2 with no capacity. At the start there are 5 singletons, and cut = all the weight = 18.

StepClustersCandidates (affinity)Chosencut
0A, B, C, D, E——18
1A, B, C, D, EA–B 5, D–E 5, B–C 4, C–D 2, A–C 1, C–E 1A–B (tie, A < D)18 − 5 = 13
2AB, C, D, EAB–C 1+4=5, D–E 5, C–D 2, C–E 1AB–C (tie, [A,B] < [D])13 − 5 = 8

Step 2 is the key: once A and B contract into one supernode, A–C (1) and B–C (4) are two parallel wires to C, and together AB–C = 5. The original author drew this step specially, and this course’s films and lab keep it.

The count reaches 2: stop. The result is ABC | DE, and the remaining cut is C–D (2) and C–E (1), 3 in all.

§ 5A preview of capacity, and the stopping state

Suppose each cluster can hold at most C units of size (here every node has size 1):

  • Capacity 3: AB+C has size 3 ≤ 3, legal, and the path is the same as above, giving ABC | DE.
  • Capacity 2: step 2’s AB+C has size 3 > 2 and is blocked; the next legal candidate is D–E, which merges. That leaves AB, C and DE: AB+C is 3 and so is C+DE, both over capacity, so no legal pair with positive affinity remains. Stop.

With capacity 2 the result is AB | C | DE, 3 clusters, cut 8, K = 2 not reached. This is not a program error but what the rule demands: with no legal merge, stop and say so honestly. In Module 04 this becomes the main topic: telling “simply impossible” apart from “greedy walked into a dead end”.

Report three states separately

Legal (capacity compliant): no cluster is over capacity. The capacity-2 result is legal. Target reached: the number of clusters is ≤ K. The capacity-2 result does not reach it. Stop reason: target-reached, capacity-blocked (positive pairs remain but all are over capacity) or no-positive-affinity (the graph is disconnected and no wire joins the remaining clusters).

§ 6The pseudocode, line by line

  1. clusters ← one singleton per node
  2. while |clusters| > K:
  3. cands ← every cluster pair (P, Q) with affinity > 0
  4. legal ← those in cands with size(P) + size(Q) ≤ C
  5. if legal is empty: stop (say K is not reached)
  6. (P, Q) ← the legal pair with the smallest key (−affinity, P, Q)
  7. replace P and Q with P ∪ Q
  8. return clusters, cut, whether K is reached, stop reason

Click any line for its explanation. When you step through the lab, the line being executed lights up.

How it maps to the Python: the selection key on line 6 → selection_key(c); lines 2–8 → the main loop of greedy_pair_merge(...).

§ 7Greedy’s limits, and what they mean in practice

  • No backtracking: once merged, never split. On G01 greedy happens to reach the smallest cut, 3, but that is a property of this graph. Module 04 has a counterexample where greedy reaches cut 18 while the best legal grouping has only 11.
  • It depends on the score: this module uses “the total weight between clusters”. With Module 02’s shared neighbours or another score, the merge order and the result can change.
  • Complexity: a naive implementation checks every cluster pair at every step. Real tools usually keep a priority queue and update only the few pairs a merge affects.
  • In practice: in chip design, this kind of bottom-up merging often coarsens a huge number of cells into fewer clusters before handing them to partitioning or placement tools; merging usually also limits a cluster’s area, which is the next module’s capacity constraint.

08 / Common mistakes and limits

Where it goes wrong

Each entry gives the wrong result, the cause, a counterexample and the fix; every number in the counterexamples is recomputed by the tests.

Comparing candidate arrays as strings

Wrong result
The original page merged A–C (affinity 1) first, ending at ACDE | B, cut 9.
Why
JavaScript’s cand < best turns the arrays into strings before comparing: "-1,A,C" sorts before "-5,A,B" because the character 1 is less than 5. Character order replaces the value-first rule.
Counterexample
On the same graph with the same rule, a correct comparison gives ABC | DE, cut 3; across four settings the original page gives 9/16/12/16 and the correct values are 3/8/8/3 (this course’s tests reproduce both).
Fix
Compare field by field: numbers by value, names alphabetically. This course’s engine uses compareKeys(), with regression tests for 10 versus 2 and 2.5 versus 2.

Forcing a merge to reach K

Wrong result
With capacity 2, squeezing C into AB or DE and reporting “2 clusters”.
Why
The stopping rule is “K is reached, or no legal positive pair remains”. With no legal pair, stop and say so.
Counterexample
With capacity 2, AB + C has size 3 > 2, and so does C + DE; the correct result is AB | C | DE, 3 clusters, cut 8, targetReached = false.
Fix
Report separately: the number of clusters, whether the capacity holds, whether K is reached, and the stop reason.

Forgetting to add parallel wires after a contraction

Wrong result
After merging A and B, taking the affinity of AB–C as just 4 (only B–C).
Why
The affinity between clusters is the total of every wire between them: both A–C and B–C reach C.
Counterexample
AB–C = 1 + 4 = 5, tying D–E; counted as 4, step 2 would merge D–E first.
Fix
At every step, add up the crossing wires again for each cluster pair.

Thinking greedy always finds the best grouping

Wrong result
Seeing G01 end at cut 3 and concluding that greedy merging is optimal in general.
Why
Greedy only takes the largest pair at each step and never undoes a merge; early choices limit what can happen later.
Counterexample
Module 04’s suboptimal graph: greedy reaches cut 18, while the best legal grouping has cut 11.
Fix
Keep “this example gives 3” apart from “the algorithm is always optimal”; when you need a guarantee, compare with exhaustive search or another method.

Scope and limits

  • Greedy never backtracks: a wrong early merge cannot be corrected later (Module 04 gives a counterexample).
  • The result depends on the score and the tie rule; with another score (Module 02) the result can be entirely different.
  • A naive implementation checks every cluster pair at every step; large circuits usually use a priority queue with local updates.
  • In this module capacity is only a preview, counted as the sum of node sizes; cells of different areas get proper treatment in Module 04.
  • The course’s small graphs use invented teaching values.
