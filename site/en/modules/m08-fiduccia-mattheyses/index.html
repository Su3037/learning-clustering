01 / Goals and problem

What this module solves

Start with the problem and what counts as success, then the algorithm. Each goal links to where it is taught.

RecapModule 07’s four ideas carry over unchanged: gain, lock, trial and best prefix (the empty prefix included). Only one thing differs: KL swaps a pair at a time, FM moves one node at a time, so a balance window has to limit the side sizes.

Problem statement

Input

A weighted 2-pin graph (or a multi-pin netlist), an initial bisection, and the balance parameter maxDiff.

Output

For each pass: the trial moves (node, gain, FS/TE, cut after the move), the cumulative gains and the best prefix; then the accepted bisection, the final cut and the stop reason.

Objective

Lower the cut by single-node moves (with multi-pin nets: the total weight of the cut nets).

Constraints

After a move both sides are nonempty and |n₀ − n₁| ≤ maxDiff (counting nodes); each node moves at most once per pass.

Assumptions

Gains may be negative; take the largest gain among legal candidates, ties by node name; on a tie for the best prefix the shorter one wins; the empty prefix is allowed.

Stopping

A pass whose best prefix is empty, or the pass limit.

§ 1How it differs from KL: one node at a time

KL swaps one for one, so the side sizes never change. FM (Fiduccia and Mattheyses, 1982) instead moves one node at a time. That is more flexible, but the sizes change, so a balance constraint is added:

After a move, both sides are nonempty and |n₀ − n₁| ≤ maxDiff.

This chapter’s seed is the same as Module 07’s: AE | BCD, cut 12. Five nodes with maxDiff = 1 means the sides can only be 2 : 3 or 3 : 2.

single move

单点移动

Moving one node from its side (F) to the other side (T).

balance window

平衡窗口

In this chapter, by node count:

legal candidate

合法候选

A node that is unlocked and still satisfies the balance after moving.

§ 2Legality and gain: the largest may not be allowed to move

The gain of a single move = the cut before the move − the cut after it. Written per net (signal net), it splits into two parts:

FS (from-side): v is a net’s only pin on side F → after the move the net is no longer cut, +w. TE (to-side): a net has no pin at all on side T → after the move it becomes cut, −w.

For ordinary 2-pin edges this is just “external weight − internal weight”.

The initial gains on seed AE | BCD:

NodeSideGainSides afterLegal
Aleft (2 nodes)+61 : 4no
Bright+13 : 2yes
Cright−43 : 2yes
Dright+33 : 2yes
Eleft (2 nodes)+61 : 4no

A and E have the largest gain but may not move: moving either leaves 1 node on the left. “Largest” is compared among legal candidates only: choose D (+3).

D’s breakdown: on DE (5), D is the only pin on side F → FS +5; CD (2) has no pin on side T (the left) → TE −2; gain = 3.

§ 3One full pass: D, then A, then the roll-back

StepMoveGainCut afterCumulative
1D (right → left)+393
2A (left → right)+639
3C−257

Once D has moved, the left has 3 nodes, and only then does A become a legal candidate, with gain +6. Every gain after that is negative, but the moves are still tried one by one, until no legal unlocked node is left.

The largest cumulative gain is the first 2 steps (+9): from the start apply only “move D, move A”, and accept ABC | DE, cut 3. The final cut 12 is only where the trials ended, not the result. Pass 2 starts from ABC | DE; its best prefix is empty, and the algorithm stops.

§ 4What negative gains are for: a verified example

The original attachment has a case named hillclimb, whose text says: “initial cut 9, every legal move has gain ≤ 0; plain greedy gets stuck, while FM first takes −1, then gains +6, down to 4.” Computed from the attachment’s own data:

  • the initial cut is 18, not 9;
  • E’s move is legal, with gain +10: not every legal gain is ≤ 0;
  • pass 1’s best cut is 8, and exhaustive search over every balanced bisection gives an optimum of 8 too, not 4.
The text does not match the data

This case cannot be used to show that “negative gains help escape a local optimum”. This course keeps its data only as a regression test and as material on “how to audit an example”, not as correct teaching material.

This course’s replacement example was found by a seeded search and checked by independent computation:

  • 6 nodes, edges A–C 4, A–E 5, B–C 2, B–E 1, C–F 3, D–E 1, E–F 1; seed ADE | BCF, maxDiff = 2; initial cut 6.
  • At the start every legal move has gain ≤ 0 (A, B, C, D are −1, F is −2, E is −4): hill climbing that accepts only positive gains stops at cut 6.
  • FM first moves A to the right (−1) and B to the left (−1), and the cut rises to 8; now E’s strongest tie, A–E (5), is already across, E’s gain becomes +4, and after the move the cut is 4.
  • The best prefix is k* = 3, accepting cut 4; exhaustive search over every balanced bisection gives exactly 4.

The two steps that first make things worse change E’s surroundings, and only then does E have a positive gain. That is what “trying negative gains” is for.

§ 5Buckets, complexity and multi-pin nets

One of the FM paper’s contributions is linear time: nodes go into “buckets” by gain (an array of linked lists), a pointer tracks the largest gain, and each move updates only the affected nodes, so a pass costs time in proportion to the total pin count P.

Showing is not implementing

Both the original attachment and this course’s lab show nodes grouped by gain into buckets. This course’s engine recomputes after each move only the gains of nodes on affected nets (FM’s incremental update rule, checked one by one against a from-scratch recomputation in the tests), but choosing the largest gain still scans every legal candidate. So this implementation does not reach O(P), and the page does not claim it.

Multi-pin nets (extension): in a real hypergraph one net can join 3 or more cells. FS/TE must be decided by counting pins on each side: for net {A,B,C,D} (weight 3) with A, B, C on the left and D on the right, A’s gain is 0 (the net stays cut after A moves); split the net into a clique (3/(4−1) = 1 per pair) and count edges, and you get 1 − 2 = −1. This course’s engine implements multi-pin nets by counting pins; the attachment’s third case, named hypergraph, actually has only 2-pin edges, and this chapter treats it as an ordinary 7-node graph (initial cut 22, best prefix of 3 steps, down to cut 1).

§ 6The pseudocode, line by line

  1. repeat:
  2. count each net's pins per side; compute every gain by FS/TE
  3. while a legal unlocked node exists:
  4. v ← the legal candidate with the largest gain (ties by name)
  5. move v (a trial), lock v
  6. update only the gains of other unlocked nodes on v's nets
  7. k* ← prefix with the largest cumulative gain (0 allowed)
  8. current bisection ← start with only the first k* steps applied
  9. until k* = 0

Click any line for its explanation. When you step through the lab, the line being executed lights up.

How it maps to Python: lines 2 and 6 → gain(); lines 3–4 → legal_moves(); line 7 → best_prefix(); a whole pass → fm_pass() and fm_refine().

§ 7Where it applies

  • FM and its multilevel variants are the core refinement step of EDA partitioning tools; real tools handle multi-pin nets and area balance.
  • This chapter’s base track balances by node count only; an area window is not implemented and is marked as an extension.
  • The result depends on the seed and the tie rule. The attachment’s tie rule (on equal gain prefer moving from the larger side, names in reverse) differs from this course’s, so the move order differs in some cases; every value listed in this chapter is computed independently by this course’s engine and its Python reference, and the two are checked against each other.

07 / Code it · Python in your browser

Implement gain and the legal candidates

Nothing to install: Python runs in your browser (Pyodide) and your code stays on this device. The checker is the same check.py as in the download.

Tasks (starter.py):

  1. gain(nets, side, v): return (gain, FS list, TE list), counting each net’s pins per side; this works for multi-pin nets too.
  2. legal_moves(side, locked, max_diff): the unlocked nodes that are still balanced after moving.

Hint 1: for each net that contains v, count its pins on v’s side (F) and on the other side (T): only v on F → +w; none on T → −w.

Hint 2: use balance_ok({**side, v: 1 - side[v]}, max_diff) to decide whether a move is legal. The checker audits the attachment’s hillclimb data, and checks gain = cut before − cut after on 150 random graphs (multi-pin nets included).

08 / Common mistakes and limits

Where it goes wrong

Each entry gives the wrong result, the cause, a counterexample and the fix; every number in the counterexamples is recomputed by the tests.

Picking the node with the largest gain outright

Wrong result
On seed AE | BCD, move A first (gain +6).
Why
A is on the left, which has only 2 nodes; moving it leaves 1 there, and |1 − 4| = 3 > 1 breaks the balance.
Counterexample
The legal candidates are only B (+1), C (−4) and D (+3); choose D. Once D has moved across, A becomes a legal +6.
Fix
Filter for legality first, then take the largest gain among the legal candidates.

Copying the attachment’s hillclimb claim

Wrong result
The text: “initial cut 9, every legal move has gain ≤ 0; FM first takes −1, then gains +6, down to 4.”
Why
Computed from the attachment’s own data, the initial cut is 18; E’s move is legal with gain +10; pass 1’s best cut is 8, and the exhaustive balanced optimum is 8 too. The text does not match the data.
Counterexample
This course’s replacement: initial cut 6, every legal move has gain ≤ 0; FM takes −1, −1, then gains +4, keeps 3 steps, cut 4, and exhaustive search confirms that is the balanced optimum.
Fix
Verify an example with an independent computation (each legal move’s gain plus the exhaustive optimum) before it goes into teaching material.

Taking “buckets drawn” for “linear time implemented”

Wrong result
The page shows nodes grouped by gain into buckets, so the implementation is claimed to be O(P).
Why
The paper’s linear time comes from incrementally maintained bucket lists and a max-gain pointer; the attachment’s and this course’s teaching implementations scan every candidate at each step.
Counterexample
This course updates gains incrementally (recomputing only the nodes on affected nets), but selection is still a scan: it cannot be called O(P).
Fix
Keep “the complexity of the algorithm in theory” and “the complexity of this implementation” apart.

Calling an ordinary-edge example a hypergraph

Wrong result
The attachment’s third case has the id hypergraph, but its data are 2-pin edges only.
Why
A real multi-pin net decides FS/TE by counting its pins on each side; splitting the net into edges first gives different results.
Counterexample
Net {A,B,C,D} (weight 3) with A, B, C on the left and D on the right: counting pins, A’s gain is 0 (the net stays cut after A moves); split into a clique (3/(4−1) = 1 per pair) and counted by edges, A’s “gain” is 1 − 2 = −1.
Fix
When multi-pin nets matter, count pins (this course’s engine supports it, marked as an extension).

Thinking count balance is area balance

Wrong result
Believing maxDiff limits the area of each side.
Why
This chapter counts nodes; with cells of different areas the window has to be defined by area.
Counterexample
A cell of area 10 and a cell of area 1 count “the same” in a head-count window.
Fix
When area balance is needed, define legality by summed area instead (not implemented in this course, marked as an extension).

Scope and limits

  • The base track of this chapter is weighted 2-pin graphs; multi-pin nets are a marked extension (the engine already updates gains by counting pins). A balance window weighted by area is not implemented.
  • The teaching implementation’s selection step is a scan, so it does not reach the 1982 paper’s O(P) per pass.
  • FM is a heuristic: reaching the balanced optimum on G01 and on the replacement example is a property of those two graphs.
  • The result depends on the initial bisection and the tie rule (this course: gain descending, then node name). The attachment’s tie rule (on equal gain prefer moving from the larger side, names in reverse) differs, so the move order in some cases differs.
