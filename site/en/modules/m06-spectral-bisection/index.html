Problem statement

Input

An undirected graph with non-negative weights; a balance window (for example, 40%–60% of the nodes per group).

Output

Two groups of nodes, the cut, the Fiedler order, the cut and legality at each sweep position, and numerical diagnostics (λ, residual, connectivity, degeneracy).

Objective

Find a bisection inside the balance window with as small a cut as possible; the Fiedler order supplies a set of candidate cut positions.

Constraints

Both groups non-empty; each group’s size inside the window; cut only between neighbours in the order (n − 1 candidates).

Assumptions

Unnormalised L = D − A (D the degree matrix); a connected graph; ties in the order broken by node name; a vector and its sign flip count as the same result.

Stopping

After sweeping all n − 1 positions, take the legal position with the least cut. The spectral method gives a good candidate, not a guaranteed global optimum.

03 / Lesson · from intuition to pseudocode

From an example to pseudocode

Section numbers match the film chapters and slide pages; the full map is in the crosswalk at the end of this page.

§ 1Bisection: why “non-empty” and “balanced” must be declared

A bisection splits the nodes into two groups. If all we ask for is the smallest cut, there is a useless answer: put every node in one group, and the cut is 0. So the problem has to be stated first:

  1. Both groups are non-empty;
  2. Left and right are not distinguished: ABC | DE and DE | ABC are the same split;
  3. A balance window: each group’s size must lie in some range, for example 40%–60% of all nodes.

Under these conventions, how many splits do 5 nodes have? Of the 2⁵ = 32 ways to label every node left or right, drop “all left” and “all right”, then divide by 2 (swapping sides counts once):

(2⁵ − 2) / 2 = 15

The original quiz’s 16

The original quiz says 16. The extra one is “one group empty, the other everything”. This course enumerates the non-empty, unlabelled bisections of G01, and the tests assert exactly 15.

Enumerating 15 is fine for 5 nodes, but hopeless once there are many. The spectral idea is: give every point a number first, line them up, and cut only between neighbours on that line. With n nodes there are only n − 1 candidates.

§ 2From edge weights to the Laplacian

degree d(v)

度

The sum of the weights of the edges at v. A’s degree = 5 + 1 = 6.

adjacency matrix A

邻接矩阵

A[u][v] = w(u, v); 0 where there is no edge.

Laplacian L = D − A

拉普拉斯矩阵

Degrees on the diagonal, negative edge weights off it. The D here is the degree matrix, not the inter-cluster delay D of Module 09.

Each undirected edge (u, v, w) contributes to four entries of L: L[u][u] += w, L[v][v] += w, L[u][v] −= w, L[v][u] −= w. The L of G01 (rows and columns A–E):

ABCDE
A6−5−100
B−59−400
C−1−48−2−1
D00−27−5
E00−1−56

Two self-checks: L is symmetric, and every row sums to 0 (the degree on the diagonal equals the sum of the negative weights in its row). Row sums of 0 mean the all-ones vector satisfies L·1 = 0: λ = 0 is always an eigenvalue of L.

§ 3The quadratic form: why tightly linked points get close numbers

Give every node a number x_v. L has one key property:

xᵀLx = Σ w(u, v)·(x_u − x_v)², summed once over every undirected edge.

If x may only be +1 (left group) or −1 (right group), a cut edge contributes w·(2)² = 4w and an uncut edge contributes 0, so xᵀLx = 4·cut. The indicator vector of ABC | DE gives 4 × 3 = 12.

Minimising the cut is hard because x must be ±1. The spectral method relaxes that condition: x may be any real vector, as long as it is not constant (orthogonal to all-ones) and has fixed length. The optimum of the relaxed problem is the eigenvector of the second-smallest eigenvalue λ2.

The spring analogy (only here)

Picture each edge as a spring, stiffer for a larger weight; xᵀLx is like the total “energy” of all the stretched springs. Stiff springs pull tightly linked nodes together, so their numbers come out close. This is only a picture to help with the quadratic form: this page runs no physical simulation, the weights are not real stiffnesses, and the algorithms in other modules have nothing to do with springs.

§ 4The Fiedler vector and its conditions

eigenvalue / eigenvector

特征值 / 特征向量

A number λ and a non-zero vector x with Lx = λx.

Fiedler vector

Fiedler 向量

The eigenvector of the second-smallest eigenvalue λ2 (pronounced “FEED-ler”). λ2 is also called the algebraic connectivity.

The eigenvalues of G01 (solved live in this page’s browser, matching NumPy):

λ1 ≈ 0, λ2 ≈ 1.7733143437, λ3 ≈ 8.455, λ4 ≈ 11.605, λ5 ≈ 14.166

The Fiedler vector (sign fixed so that A is positive) is about A 0.519, B 0.418, C 0.106, D −0.489, E −0.553.

Three conditions to watch:

  • Connected: in a connected graph λ1 = 0 occurs once; when the graph falls apart, λ2 is 0 as well, and the vector only tells you which points share a connected component.
  • Degenerate: if λ2 = λ3, the second eigenvector is not unique; any direction in that eigenspace is equally valid, and the order can differ.
  • Sign flip: if x is an eigenvector, so is −x. Flipping the whole vector only reverses the order and swaps left and right; the grouping is unchanged.

§ 5The sweep: try every cut inside the window

Sort by Fiedler value, smallest first: E, D, C, B, A. Cut between neighbours, with the first k as one group:

kLeftRightcut40/60 window (2–3 a side)
1ED C B A6not legal
2E DC B A3legal
3E D CB A5legal
4E D C BA6not legal

Inside the window, k = 2 has the least cut: DE | ABC, cut 3. Two things to notice:

  • “Cutting right in the middle” (k = 3) is not better: its cut is 5.
  • On this graph the sign boundary also happens to fall at k = 2; in general the sign does not guarantee a split inside the window, which is why we sweep.

The original author’s page let students choose k by hand and see the cut live; this chapter’s lab keeps that interaction.

§ 6Three common misunderstandings, corrected

  1. The number of bisections is 15, not 16 (see section 1).
  2. Without a balance constraint, the optimum does not have to isolate one node. Isolating a node cuts exactly its degree: A 6, B 9, C 8, D 7, E 6, so at least 6; yet ABC | DE cuts only 3. The correct statement: without a constraint a group can come out too small, so declare a window.
  3. A precomputed vector is not a solver. The original page stores a vector to three decimals; changing k recomputes the cut live, but changing the graph does not recompute the vector. The residual ‖Lx − λx‖/‖x‖ of the three-decimal vector is about 0.0044; that is rounding, not a failed solve. This course solves live in the browser with Jacobi rotations (residual about 10⁻¹⁵) and keeps the original values for comparison.

§ 7Pseudocode and numerical self-checks

  1. L ← D − A
  2. (λ, x) ← eigendecomposition of L, ascending
  3. check λ1 ≈ 0, connectivity, degenerate λ2, residual
  4. f ← eigenvector of λ2, sign fixed
  5. order ← nodes by f ascending, ties by name
  6. for k = 1 … n−1: left = order[:k], compute cut
  7. return the k with least cut inside the window

Click any line for its explanation. When you step through the lab, the line being executed lights up.

How it maps to Python: line 1 → laplacian(); line 2 → the provided jacobi_eigen(); lines 6–7 → sweep() and spectral_bisection(). The checker also verifies the residual, sign-flip equivalence, and λ2 = 0 on a disconnected graph.

In EDA, spectral methods often give large partitioning problems a first solution with a global view, which is then handed to local improvement methods such as KL/FM: exactly the next two modules.

Build L, sweep the cuts, self-check the numerics

08 / Common mistakes and limits

Where it goes wrong

Each entry gives the wrong result, the cause, a counterexample and the fix; every number in the counterexamples is recomputed by the tests.

Counting 16 bisections of 5 nodes

Wrong result
The original quiz: “5 nodes split into two groups in 16 ways”.
Why
Of the 2⁵ = 32 left/right labellings, 2 leave a group empty; dividing by 2 for swapped sides gives (32 − 2) / 2 = 15. The 16 also counts the empty-set/everything pair.
Counterexample
Enumerating the non-empty, unlabelled bisections of G01 gives exactly 15 (asserted by this course’s tests).
Fix
State “both groups non-empty, sides not distinguished” first, then count.

Thinking that without a balance constraint the optimum isolates one node

Wrong result
The original text: “with no balance constraint, the answer always cuts off one node”.
Why
Isolating a node cuts exactly its degree; the least single-node cut on this graph is 6, while ABC | DE is only 3.
Counterexample
G01’s least unconstrained cut is ABC | DE = 3, not a single node.
Fix
Say “a group can come out too small” instead, and so declare a balance window.

Taking the sign of the Fiedler vector as a balanced split

Wrong result
Cutting directly by sign and assuming both sides come out about equal.
Why
The sign is just one threshold; it does not keep the group sizes inside the window, and it does not replace the sweep.
Counterexample
On G01 the sign happens to cut at k = 2; on another graph it can cut 1 : n−1.
Fix
Sweep every position along the order and choose the least cut among positions inside the window.

Taking a stored three-decimal vector for a solver

Wrong result
The original page recomputes the cut when k changes, but the eigenvector is hard-coded to three decimals; after the graph changes the old vector is still used.
Why
The vector was precomputed and is not recomputed when the graph changes; its residual of about 0.0044 is only good enough for display.
Counterexample
This course’s live solve has a residual of about 10⁻¹⁵; the stored vector’s is 0.0044. On G01 both give the same order.
Fix
Label it “precomputed”, or, as this course does, really solve it in the browser and show the residual.

Thinking a sign flip changes the result

Wrong result
Two runs give vectors of opposite sign, so the groupings are taken to differ.
Why
If x is an eigenvector, so is −x; the order reverses as a whole, and the same cut just swaps the names of its sides.
Counterexample
On G01, x and −x both give DE | ABC, cut 3.
Fix
Compare groupings without distinguishing left and right.

Scope and limits

  • Spectral bisection gives a “good candidate”, not a guaranteed global optimum; it is often the initial solution handed to Modules 07/08 to improve.
  • This chapter uses the unnormalised L; variants such as the normalised Laplacian give different orders.
  • When the graph is disconnected, λ2 = 0 and the eigenvector only reflects the components; when λ2 is degenerate, the direction is not unique.
  • The in-browser Jacobi solver suits teaching sizes (this page is limited to 40 nodes); large problems use sparse iterative methods (such as Lanczos).
  • The spring metaphor only helps with the quadratic form; this page runs no physical simulation.
